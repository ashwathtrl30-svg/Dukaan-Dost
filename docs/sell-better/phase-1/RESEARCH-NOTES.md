# Sell Better — Phase 1 Research Notes

**Purpose:** Record the evidence used to shape the initial curriculum and distinguish research-backed principles from authored examples.  
**Status:** Desk research only; not a substitute for shopkeeper interviews or practitioner review.

## 1. Sources and how they inform the curriculum

### S1 — Indian kirana strategy, trust and reciprocity
Atul Arun Pathak and George Mathew Kandathil (2020), “Strategizing in small informal retailers in India: Home delivery as a strategic practice,” *Asia Pacific Journal of Management*, 37, 851–877.  
https://doi.org/10.1007/s10490-019-09662-4  
Accessible bibliographic record: https://ideas.repec.org/a/kap/asiapa/v37y2020i3d10.1007_s10490-019-09662-4.html

**Method / finding:** A multi-year case study using grounded-theory analysis examines Indian family-owned kirana retailers. It discusses home delivery and informal customer credit as practices embedded in trust and reciprocity.

**Curriculum use:** Supports including trust, service, home delivery and credit-boundary situations in kirana learning.

**Limitations:** This is a focused study of kirana practices, not proof that every Indian customer values the same services or that a particular practice increases profit. It does not establish a universal credit policy.

### S2 — Mumbai kirana reporting and customer relationships
Anjali Palod (10 September 2024), “How Small Kirana Shops Are Innovating To Fight Quick Commerce Giants,” *The Core*.  
https://www.thecore.in/economy/kirana-shops-quick-commerce-762196

**Method / finding:** Reported examples from Mumbai shopkeepers and customers describe delivery, regular-customer relationships, product quality, WhatsApp updates and the operational limits of competing on convenience.

**Curriculum use:** Helps ground examples in Mumbai independent retail contexts and shows why customer relationships and service choices are practical topics.

**Limitations:** Journalism and selected examples, not a representative survey. Individual reported sales changes and customer statements must not be generalized to all shops.

### S3 — Observation of Indian urban retail interactions
Nikhilesh Dholakia and Piyush Kumar Sinha (2005), “Observations on Observation in India's Dynamic Urban Markets,” *Forum Qualitative Sozialforschung / Forum: Qualitative Social Research*, 6(1), Art. 13.  
https://doi.org/10.17169/fqs-6.1.519  
Full text: https://www.qualitative-research.net/index.php/fqs/article/download/519/1125/1677

**Method / finding:** Discusses qualitative observation of shoppers and shopkeeper interactions in changing Indian urban retail settings, including the importance and difficulty of understanding real conversations and context.

**Curriculum use:** Supports designing situations around actual interaction context and treating field observation carefully rather than relying on stereotypes or assumptions.

**Limitations:** Published in 2005; retail formats and technology have changed. It is methodological and contextual, not a current estimate of customer behaviour.

### S5 — Food acquisition decisions in peri-urban Hyderabad
C. Turner et al. (2022), “Drivers of food acquisition practices in the food environment of peri-urban Hyderabad, India: A qualitative investigation,” *Health & Place*, 74, 102763.  
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.healthplace.2022.102763  
Open full text: https://researchonline.lshtm.ac.uk/id/eprint/4665978/1/1-s2.0-S1353829222000247-main.pdf

**Method / finding:** A qualitative multi-method study including interviews, participatory mapping and focus-group material. It identifies price/affordability, vendor and product properties such as quality/freshness, and social capital as factors in food acquisition.

**Curriculum use:** Informs the produce scenario's attention to freshness, affordability, quantity and customer context.

**Limitations:** Peri-urban Hyderabad study, not a universal profile of Indian shoppers. The study concerns food acquisition decisions, not a direct test of sales coaching.

### S9 — Service recovery behaviours
Hui Liao (2007), “Do it right this time: the role of employee service recovery performance in customer-perceived justice and customer loyalty after service failures,” *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 92(2), 475–489.  
https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17371092/

**Method / finding:** A field study and laboratory study examine complaint-handling behaviours. Apology, problem solving, courtesy and prompt handling were associated with customer-perceived justice and satisfaction; effects varied with failure severity and repeated failures.

**Curriculum use:** Informs the complaint lessons' emphasis on listening, courteous handling, fact-finding and realistic problem-solving.

**Limitations:** Not specific to Indian micro-retailers. It does not justify promising that complaint handling guarantees loyalty or repeat purchase.

## 2. Learning design evidence

### L1 — Retrieval practice versus elaborative encoding
Ariel de Oliveira Gonçalves, Bruno Felipe Barbosa Muniz and Antônio Jaeger (2025), “Retrieval Practice Versus Elaborative Encoding: A Systematic and Meta-analytic Review,” *Educational Psychology Review*.  
https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-10076-6

**Finding:** The review included 44 empirical studies and 142 comparisons. It reported a small overall advantage for retrieval practice over elaborative conditions (g = 0.14), with the advantage conditional on corrective feedback; without feedback, elaborative approaches could perform better.

**Curriculum use:** The learner should attempt an answer before receiving the explanation, but the attempt must be followed by useful, corrective teaching feedback. The product should not mistake asking a question alone for effective learning.

**Limitations:** General learning research, not a trial of this exact product, adult shopkeepers, or Indian retail learning. Effect sizes do not predict business outcomes.

### L2 — Meta-analysis of learning techniques
Gregory M. Donoghue and John A. C. Hattie (2021), “A Meta-Analysis of Ten Learning Techniques,” *Frontiers in Education*.  
https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2021.581216

**Finding:** A meta-analysis of 242 studies, 1,619 effects and 169,179 participants found distributed practice and practice testing among the strongest techniques reviewed. The authors caution that many outcomes were surface/factual and that transfer, feedback and learning domain matter.

**Curriculum use:** Supports revisiting ideas over time and including a learner-generated response, while requiring a separate test of whether the learner can apply an idea in a changed shop situation.

**Limitations:** Does not prove that a 45–90 second lesson or any particular app format will improve shop performance.

## 3. What the evidence does — and does not — justify

| Design decision | Evidence status |
|---|---|
| Use real Indian retail situations | Supported as a context-sensitive design choice by Indian retail field research; exact scenarios still need field validation. |
| Ask the learner to think before showing the explanation | Consistent with retrieval/self-explanation learning research; not a direct trial of Sell Better. |
| Always provide useful corrective teaching after the response | Strongly supported by learning research; exact feedback wording must be tested. |
| Revisit a principle in a new situation later | Reasonable application of distributed practice and transfer principles; product effectiveness must be measured. |
| Keep lessons short | Product usability hypothesis, not established as universally optimal by the cited sources. |
| Claim increased sales/profit/retention | Not justified by these sources; do not make such claims. |
| Assume customer behaviour by age, gender, region or language | Not justified; do not encode stereotypes. |

## 4. Research gaps to close with fieldwork

1. Ask independent shopkeepers which customer situations occur often enough to be worth practising.
2. Ask whether the wording reflects how they actually speak and work.
3. Validate whether the advice is practical during a busy shift.
4. Review price, credit, return, tailoring, salon and hardware examples with practitioners.
5. Validate Hindi and Hinglish with native speakers and shopkeepers, not only machine translation.
6. Test whether a learner can apply the principle to a different situation after a delay.

## 5. Editorial rule

Sources inform the principles and context; they do not validate each fictional lesson. Keep lesson records in draft status until shopkeepers, practitioners and language reviewers have actually reviewed them. Never turn a small case study, one interview or a news anecdote into a universal claim about Indian customers.
